Module 07: Spatial Epidemiology

Spatial Autocorrelation & Moran’s I

Learning objectives - Understand spatial autocorrelation: Tobler’s First Law of Geography - Build spatial weights matrices (queen contiguity, rook, distance-based, k-NN) - Compute Global Moran’s I and its significance via analytic tests - Calculate Local Moran’s I (LISA) to identify spatial clusters - Produce cluster maps: High-High, Low-Low, High-Low, Low-High quadrants - Interpret Moran scatterplots for health outcomes - Compare LISA with Getis-Ord G* hot-spot analysis

Data: Data/usa_data.csv — CDC adult diabetes prevalence and County Health Rankings covariates for 3,108 US counties (CONUS + DC)
Geometries: Data/US_COUNTY.shp — county polygons in NAD83 Albers (EPSG:5070)
Estimated time: 3 hours | Level: Advanced
Libraries: sf, spdep, ggplot2, dplyr, patchwork

2. Spatial Weights Matrix

A spatial weights matrix W encodes the neighbourhood structure: - Queen contiguity: shares any boundary point or corner - Rook contiguity: shares an edge only - Distance-based: \(w_{ij} = 1\) if distance \(<\) threshold - k-NN: each unit connected to its \(k\) nearest neighbours - Inverse distance: \(w_{ij} = 1/d_{ij}\) (continuous)

Row-standardisation: \(W^*\) where each row sums to 1 (so \(W^*x\) = spatial lag of \(x\)).

With 3,108 counties we never materialise a dense \(n \times n\) matrix. spdep stores neighbours as an nb list and row-standardised weights as a listw object. Isolated island counties (no contiguous neighbour) are retained with zero.policy = TRUE: their spatial lag is set to 0 rather than NA.

3. Global Moran’s I

What is Global Moran’s I? (Math for Dummies)

Global Moran’s I is a single number that tells you whether places on a map with high (or low) values are clustered together, or scattered randomly.

Math intuition (for dummies!):

  • Imagine you have values for every county, like the adult diabetes prevalence.
  • For each county, look at its value and the values of its neighbours (using a weights matrix \(W\)).
  • Moran’s I compares:
    • Are high values near other high values, and low near low? (clustering, Moran’s I \(> 0\))
    • Are high and low values mixed up? (random, Moran’s I \(\approx 0\))
    • Are high values near low, and vice versa? (checkerboard, Moran’s I \(< 0\))

The “for dummies” math:

  1. First, for each county, subtract the average rate from its value. This gives you “how much above or below average is this county?” Call this \(z\).
  2. Do the same for all counties so you have a list of \(z\)’s.
  3. Look up the neighbours for each county (using \(W\)), and see if a county’s \(z\) is similar to its neighbours’ \(z\)’s.
  4. Moran’s I formula looks like:

\[ I = \frac{N}{\sum W} \times \frac{\sum_i z_i \left( \sum_j W_{ij} z_j \right)}{\sum_i z_i^2} \]

  • \(N\): Number of counties

  • \(W\): The matrix showing who’s neighbours with whom

  • \(z\): Values after subtracting the mean

  • If “like is near like” a lot, \(I\) is positive and big.

  • If there’s no pattern, \(I\) is close to zero.

  • If opposites are neighbours, \(I\) is negative.

In short: Moran’s I = “Do neighbours tend to be similar, or not?”

We use spdep::moran.test() (analytic randomisation moments) for the multi-variable table. A permutation histogram for diabetes uses moran.mc() with nsim = 199 — 999 draws are more precise but slow on 3,108 counties; 199 is enough to see that the observed \(I\) sits far in the tail.

moran_one <- function(x, lw, label) {
  mt <- moran.test(x, lw, zero.policy = TRUE, alternative = "two.sided")
  tibble(
    variable = label,
    I        = unname(mt$estimate["Moran I statistic"]),
    E_I      = unname(mt$estimate["Expectation"]),
    z_score  = unname(mt$statistic),
    p_value  = mt$p.value
  )
}

The Global Moran’s I statistic quantifies the overall spatial autocorrelation of the variable across the study area.

  • A positive and significant Moran’s I (\(p < 0.05\)) indicates that areas with high (or low) values cluster together — there is spatial clustering of similar values (either hot spots or cold spots).
  • A negative Moran’s I suggests spatial dispersion — neighbouring areas tend to have dissimilar values.
  • A value near zero (and a non-significant \(p\)-value) indicates a random spatial pattern, with no evidence of spatial autocorrelation.

In the results above, several variables — diabetes prevalence, obesity, percent aged 65+, food environment index, PCP count, uninsured count, and SPR — typically show a positive and statistically significant Moran’s I. Counties with high values tend to sit next to other high-value counties (and low next to low). Large \(z\)-scores confirm that this clustering is unlikely to be chance. This is the quantitative version of Tobler’s First Law applied to county health data.

4. Local Moran’s I (LISA)

Local Moran’s I (LISA) helps us find out where on the map we have clusters of high or low values — for example, groups of counties with similarly high diabetes prevalence (hot spots) or low rates (cold spots).

In simplest terms:

  • For each county, LISA compares its value (diabetes prevalence) to its neighbours.
  • It tells us if a county is surrounded by similar values (“cluster”) or very different ones (“outlier”).
  • We can map these results to see local hot/cold spots.

The math, made simple. For each county \(i\):

\[ I_i = \dfrac{(x_i - \bar{x})}{s^2} \sum_j w_{ij}(x_j - \bar{x}) \]

  • \(x_i\) = value at county \(i\)
  • \(\bar{x}\) = average value across all counties
  • \(w_{ij}\) = weight between county \(i\) and neighbour \(j\) (1 if neighbour, 0 if not, then row-standardised)
  • \(s^2\) = variance of all values

Interpretation:

  • High-High (HH): County and neighbours have high values (hot spot)
  • Low-Low (LL): County and neighbours have low values (cold spot)
  • High-Low (HL): High-value county among low-value neighbours (outlier)
  • Low-High (LH): Low-value county among high-value neighbours (outlier)
  • NS: Not significant (no clear cluster/outlier)

In practical terms, LISA tells us not just if spatial clustering exists (like Moran’s I), but where those clusters or outliers are on the map.

lisa <- localmoran(gdf$diabetes_prev, lw_queen, zero.policy = TRUE)
gdf$lisa_i <- lisa[, "Ii"]
# Column name is stable across recent spdep versions
pcol <- grep("^Pr\\(", colnames(lisa), value = TRUE)[1]

  z_std > 0 & z_lag > 0 ~ "HH",

The Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) cluster map reveals spatial patterns in county-level diabetes prevalence:

  • HH (High-High): “Hot spot” counties with high diabetes rates surrounded by high-rate neighbours. These are potential priority regions for intervention.

5. Bivariate Local Moran’s I

The Bivariate Local Moran’s I asks whether the value of one variable at a location (diabetes in county \(i\)) is related to the values of a different variable in neighbouring locations (obesity in nearby counties).

What does the bivariate statistic measure? - Are high diabetes rates in county \(A\) surrounded by high obesity rates in neighbouring counties? - Or are low diabetes rates surrounded by low obesity nearby? - Or is there a mismatch between the two variables?

Steps:

  1. Choose two variables:
    • \(x\): Value at the target location (diabetes prevalence in county \(i\))
    • \(y\): Value in each neighbouring location (obesity in surrounding counties)
  2. Standardise both variables:
    • Subtract each variable’s mean and divide by its standard deviation.
    • This gives both variables a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1.
    • \[ z_{x,i} = \frac{x_i - \overline{x}}{s_x} \]
    • \[ z_{y,j} = \frac{y_j - \overline{y}}{s_y} \]
  3. For each county \(i\), calculate the bivariate Local Moran’s I:
    • \[ I_i = z_{x,i} \times \left( \sum_j w_{ij} \cdot z_{y,j} \right) \]
    • Where \(w_{ij}\) = spatial weight between county \(i\) and neighbour \(j\).
    • Translation: “How much is diabetes in county \(i\) related to the (weighted) average obesity in its neighbours?”
  4. What does the value mean?
    • Large positive \(I_i\):
      • County \(i\) has high diabetes, surrounded by neighbours with high obesity (High-High cluster), or
      • County \(i\) has low diabetes, surrounded by low-obesity neighbours (Low-Low cluster).
    • Large negative \(I_i\):
      • County \(i\) is an outlier: for example, low diabetes in county \(i\), but high obesity in neighbours (or vice versa).
  5. Easy analogy:
    • Think of checking whether a high-diabetes county sits in a high-obesity neighbourhood — or whether it stands out as an exception among its neighbours.

Bivariate Local Moran’s I shows whether the value of one variable at a location (diabetes) is spatially associated with the value of a different variable nearby (obesity). It also reveals where these connections or mismatches occur on your map.

spdep::localmoran_bv() requires every county to have at least one neighbour, but two CONUS island counties have none under queen contiguity. We therefore compute \(I_i = z_{x,i} \cdot (W z_y)\) with lag.listw(..., zero.policy = TRUE) and a local permutation of \(z_y\) (nsim = 199). Island counties get lag 0.

zx  <- as.numeric(scale(gdf$diabetes_prev))

The bivariate Local Moran’s I map colours each county by cluster type:

  • HH (High-High): Counties with high diabetes surrounded by neighbours with high obesity — spatial “hot spots” for both variables.
  • LL (Low-Low): Counties with low diabetes surrounded by low-obesity neighbours — “cold spots.”
  • HL (High-Low): High diabetes but neighbours with low obesity — spatial outliers.
  • LH (Low-High): Low diabetes but neighbours with high obesity — the reverse outlier.
  • NS (Not Significant): No significant spatial association at \(p < 0.05\).

These clusters highlight places where diabetes and obesity reinforce each other geographically, and places that mismatch — useful for targeting interventions or investigating unusual regions.

6. Getis-Ord G* Hot Spot Analysis

The Getis-Ord G* statistic finds “hot spots” (areas surrounded by other high-value areas) and “cold spots” (areas surrounded by other low-value areas).

How does it work?

Imagine you want to know if a county with high diabetes is part of a hot spot (a cluster of high rates) or just a single high county in a sea of lower neighbours.

Step-by-step:

  1. Pick your variable: diabetes prevalence in each county.

  2. For each county (\(i\)):

    • Add up the rates in its “neighbourhood”: itself plus all its neighbours.
    • (This is different from Moran’s I, which skips itself!)

  3. Compare this local sum to the overall average:

    • If the total for that area is much higher than average, it’s a hot spot.
    • If it’s much lower, it’s a cold spot.

  4. The actual G* statistic:

    • It tells you (in standard-deviation units) how far above or below average this area’s sum is compared to chance.
    • Big positive G* = hot spot (lots of high values together).
    • Big negative G* = cold spot (lots of low values together).

Key idea: G* finds places where clusters of high or low values occur more often than you’d expect just by chance.

Example: “If my county plus my neighbours have much higher diabetes together than most, I show up as a hot spot.”

Getis-Ord G* vs LISA (Local Moran’s I):

  • LISA: Looks for spots where a place is “like its neighbours” (clusters) and places that are “unlike their neighbours” (outliers). The neighbourhood excludes the county itself.
  • Getis-Ord G*: Pinpoints hot spots or cold spots — but not outliers. The neighbourhood includes the county itself (include.self()).
  • LISA asks: “Is my county’s value surprisingly high/low compared to my neighbours? Am I part of a cluster, or an outlier?”
  • G* asks: “Do my county and its neighbours together have a sum much bigger/smaller than average?” Outliers (a high surrounded by lows) are not flagged.

Use LISA when you want clusters and outliers; use G* when you want a simple hot/cold targeting map.

# G* includes self; binary (non-standardised) weights
nb_gstar <- include.self(nb_queen)
