Module 07: Spatial Epidemiology

Spatial Regression: Lag, Error & GWR

Learning objectives - Diagnose spatial autocorrelation in OLS residuals with Lagrange Multiplier tests - Fit Spatial Lag Model (SLM): includes spatially lagged outcome - Fit Spatial Error Model (SEM): includes spatially autocorrelated errors - Compare model fit with AIC, log-likelihood, and pseudo-R² - Compute geographically weighted correlation between diabetes and obesity - Fit Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) and map spatially varying coefficients

Data: Data/usa_data.csv — CDC adult diabetes prevalence and County Health Rankings covariates for 3,108 US counties (CONUS + DC)
Geometries: Data/US_COUNTY.shp — county polygons in NAD83 Albers (EPSG:5070)
Estimated time: 3 hours | Level: Advanced
Libraries: sf, spdep, spatialreg, GWmodel, ggplot2, broom, patchwork

2. OLS Baseline and Diagnostic Tests

OLS model: \(\mathrm{diabetes}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \mathrm{obesity}_i + \beta_2 \cdot (\%\ \mathrm{aged\ 65+})_i + \beta_3 \cdot \mathrm{uninsured}_i + \varepsilon_i\)

Obesity is the focal behavioural risk factor. Percent aged 65+ adjusts for age structure. Uninsured count is a County Health Rankings access/size proxy. (Do not put SPR on the right-hand side: SPR is diabetes / national mean, a rescaling of the outcome.)

Spatial diagnostics: - Moran’s I on residuals: global test for residual autocorrelation - Lagrange Multiplier (LM) Lag test: tests if spatial lag of \(Y\) belongs in the model - LM Error test: tests if spatial error structure is needed - Robust LM tests: account for presence of the other spatial effect

Decision rule (Anselin 1988; Anselin et al. 1996):

  • If only the LM lag test is significant → fit a Spatial Lag Model.
  • If only the LM error test is significant → fit a Spatial Error Model.
  • If both are significant, compare the robust LM statistics and choose the larger one.
  • If neither is significant, OLS is adequate (no residual spatial structure after covariates).

3. Spatial Regression

Sometimes, data from nearby locations are similar just because they are close to each other. Ordinary regression (\(y = X\beta + \mathrm{error}\)) assumes observations are independent. With maps and spatial data, that is often not true.

Spatial regression methods fix this by adding geography into the regression model.

There are two main types:

  1. Spatial Lag Model (SLM)
    • This model assumes your outcome (\(y\)) depends not just on your own characteristics (\(X\)), but also on the outcome in nearby areas.
  2. Spatial Error Model (SEM)
    • Instead of \(y\) depending on neighbours, this model says the errors are spatially correlated. If the regression misses something in one area, it probably misses it nearby too.

So, spatial regression makes sure you’re not ignoring geography, and tries to avoid the problem of “neighbours being similar just because they’re neighbours”.

(In simple terms, imagine house prices: your house value depends on your house’s properties and on the prices of houses nearby. The same logic applies to county diabetes rates.)

We fit both models with spatialreg (maximum likelihood). Do not grid-search \(\rho\) or \(\lambda\) by forming dense \(|I - \rho W|\) determinants on 3,108 counties — that is \(O(n^3)\) per candidate and is exactly what lagsarlm() / errorsarlm() already do efficiently.

3.1 Spatial Lag Model (SLM)

The Spatial Lag Model (SLM) understands that things nearby tend to influence each other.

Imagine you want to predict diabetes prevalence in different counties. In normal regression, we assume each county is independent. But what if nearby counties’ rates affect each other?

The SLM makes your prediction depend on both the usual factors and the values in surrounding places. Think: a county’s diabetes rate depends not only on its own obesity and age structure, but also on diabetes in neighbouring counties (spillover, shared culture, shared food environment).

The SLM equation:

\[ y = \rho\, W y + X\beta + \varepsilon \]

Where:

  • \(y\) = outcome (diabetes prevalence)
  • \(X\) = predictors (obesity, % aged 65+, uninsured)
  • \(\beta\) = regression coefficients
  • \(W\) = spatial weights (who is a neighbour)
  • \(\rho\) (rho) = how much neighbours’ values matter
  • \(Wy\) = spatial lag (“neighbourhood average” of \(y\))
  • \(\varepsilon\) = residual noise

In plain language: “My value is influenced by my neighbours’ values, plus my own characteristics, plus some randomness.”

# SLM: y = rho W y + X beta + e  — spatialreg::lagsarlm
# method = "Matrix" avoids a dense eigen-decomposition of the 3108 × 3108 weights
slm <- lagsarlm(
  diabetes_prev ~ pct_obesity + pct_65plus + n_uninsured,
  data = gdf, listw = lw, zero.policy = TRUE, method = "Matrix", quiet = TRUE
)
print(summary(slm, Nagelkerke = TRUE))

3.2 Spatial Error Model (SEM)

The Spatial Error Model (SEM) accounts for spatial autocorrelation in the errors — when the unexplained parts of your model (the residuals) look similar for nearby locations.

The basic regression model is:

\[ Y = X\beta + u \]

where:

  • \(Y\) is your outcome
  • \(X\beta\) is the effect of your predictors
  • \(u\) is the error (what’s left unexplained)

In regular regression, we assume \(u\) is just random noise. With spatial data, errors in one place are often similar to errors nearby.

SEM models this by letting:

\[ u = \lambda W u + e \]

where:

  • \(\lambda\) is the spatial error parameter (how much errors “spread” across space)
  • \(W\) is the spatial weights matrix
  • \(e\) is well-behaved noise, but now the composite error depends on neighbouring errors

In other words, the error for each location is “contagious” — if your neighbours have high residuals, yours probably will too. SEM helps so that estimates of \(\beta\) (the effect of obesity, age, uninsured) are not biased by that spatial leftover.

Math for dummies:

  • If \(\lambda = 0\): no spatial effect; it is just regular regression.
  • If \(|\lambda|\) is big: there is a lot of “spatial noise”, and we need to correct for it.
  • The computer searches for the \(\lambda\) that makes the observed data most likely (maximum likelihood).
# SEM: Y = X beta + u, u = lambda W u + e  — spatialreg::errorsarlm
sem <- errorsarlm(
  diabetes_prev ~ pct_obesity + pct_65plus + n_uninsured,
  data = gdf, listw = lw, zero.policy = TRUE, method = "Matrix", quiet = TRUE
)
print(summary(sem, Nagelkerke = TRUE))

5. Geographically Weighted Correlation Analysis

Geographically Weighted Correlation (GW Correlation) helps us understand how the relationship between two variables (diabetes and obesity rates) changes from place to place.

For each location (a county), we:

  1. Look at nearby places: We give more weight to locations that are “close” and less to those that are “far” using a smooth weighting function (usually a Gaussian, or bell-shaped, curve).
    • The weight for each location depends on its distance to our location of interest.
    • For distance \(d\) between locations, weight: \[ w = \exp\left( -\frac{d^2}{2h^2} \right) \]
    • Here, \(h\) controls how quickly the weight drops off with distance (the bandwidth).
  2. Use these weights to get a “local” Pearson correlation:
    • The normal (global) Pearson correlation measures how two variables change together everywhere.
    • The “local” version uses our calculated weights, so it pays more attention to nearby points:
      • Local mean for variable 1: \[ \mu_1 = \sum w_i x_i \]
      • Local mean for variable 2: \[ \mu_2 = \sum w_i y_i \]
      • Local correlation: \[ r = \frac{ \sum w_i (x_i - \mu_1)(y_i - \mu_2) } { \sqrt{ \sum w_i (x_i - \mu_1)^2 } \cdot \sqrt{ \sum w_i (y_i - \mu_2)^2 } } \]

For every location, we get a local correlation that tells us how strongly the two variables move together around that spot. This can reveal spatial patterns hidden by the overall (global) correlation.

We use an adaptive Gaussian kernel with \(k = 50\) nearest neighbours on Albers centroids. A \(3108 \times 3108\) distance matrix is about 77 MB — acceptable. (bw.gwr / gwss AICc search on all counties is much slower, so we fix \(k\) rather than optimise it.)

# Adaptive Gaussian GW correlation (k = 50); one n×n distance matrix, then matrix products

If local \(r\) is high in the Mississippi Delta but weaker in the Mountain West, a single national correlation is hiding regional structure — which is exactly the motivation for GWR next.

6. Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR)

Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) is a way to see how the relationship between two things (like diabetes and obesity) changes from place to place.

Imagine you want to know, “Does living in different counties change how much one thing affects another?”

GWR works by doing a separate regression for every location, but when looking at each place, it gives more importance (“weight”) to data points that are nearby, and less weight to far-away points.

In regular regression, we try to find the best line to predict \(y\) using \(x\) (like: \(y = a + b \cdot x\)). In GWR, we let the slope and intercept (\(a\) and \(b\)) be different in every location.

The main idea: - For each place (county \(i\)), we fit a regression using all the data, but give the most weight to data closest to county \(i\). - We do this for every place, so every location gets its own “local” regression line.

The math part (for dummies): - At each spot \(i\), we find the line: \(y = a_i + b_i x\) - \(a_i\) and \(b_i\) are special to spot \(i\) (they change from place to place). - To make \(a_i\) and \(b_i\) focus on county \(i\), we use weights: nearby counties get high weights, far ones low weights. - These weights are often made using a Gaussian (bell curve). So two counties close together matter a lot; two far apart, barely matter at all.

In symbols: For each location \(i\): \[ y_j \approx a_i + b_i x_j \] (for all \(j\), but with weights based on distance from \(i\))

  • The weights \(w_{ij}\) get smaller as the distance between \(i\) and \(j\) grows: \[ w_{ij} = \exp\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^2}{2h^2}\right) \] where \(d_{ij}\) is the distance between \(i\) and \(j\), and \(h\) controls “how local” you want to be.

The full GWR model with several covariates is:

\[ y_i = \beta_0(u_i, v_i) + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j(u_i, v_i)\, X_{ij} + \varepsilon_i \]

Bandwidth. GWmodel::bw.gwr(..., approach = "AIC") searches for the AICc-optimal adaptive bandwidth. On 3,108 counties that search is very slow, so we fix an adaptive bandwidth of 50 nearest neighbours and note the choice. Smaller \(k\) → bumpier local coefficients (risk of overfitting); larger \(k\) → smoother surfaces that approach global OLS.

A wide range in the local obesity coefficient can reflect genuine regional differences in how obesity relates to diabetes (food systems, health-care access, age mixing) — or it can be a spatial artefact of noisy local fits. Always read GWR maps against the global OLS value and against whether the pattern is geographically coherent.

7. Knowledge Check

  1. The spatial lag model’s \(\rho\) parameter quantifies the extent to which diabetes prevalence in one county is associated with values in neighbouring counties, beyond what is explained by covariates. How would you interpret a \(\rho\) of about 0.4 clinically?
  2. When should you prefer the Spatial Error Model (SEM) over the Spatial Lag Model (SLM)?
  3. GWR shows the obesity coefficient varying widely across counties (for example from near 0 to well above the global OLS value). How would you decide whether this is true effect modification or a spatial artefact?
  4. Why can the obesity coefficient shrink when you move from OLS to SLM?
  5. Bandwidth acts as a smoothing parameter. What happens to local GWR coefficients if you use \(k = 20\) nearest neighbours versus \(k = 150\)?
